\section{The handoff --- the model returns}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Where we are --- both lineages threw the model away}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{center}\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{r|c|c}
 & \textbf{OR / Dynamic Programming} & \textbf{Control Theory} \\
\hline
\textbf{Model-based} & MDP \& DP \scriptsize(Lec.\ 7) & Optimal Control \scriptsize(Lec.\ 9)\\
\hline
\textbf{Data-driven} & Value-Based RL \scriptsize(Lec.\ 8) & Policy-Based RL \scriptsize(Lec.\ 10)\\
\hline
\multicolumn{3}{c}{\cellcolor{mDarkTeal!15}\textbf{Model-Based RL} \scriptsize(\alert{Lec.\ 11}) --- learn the model, reunite the four}\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\smallskip

Lectures 8 and 10 deleted their models to learn from data --- and paid for it in \alert{sample inefficiency}: model-free RL can need millions of interactions, because each transition is used once and thrown away.
\smallskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
\alert{So put the model back --- but \emph{learned}.}
\end{center}
A learned dynamics model $f_\theta$ can be replayed indefinitely; on top of it we can run \emph{planning} (DP, Lecture 7) and \emph{control} (optimal control, Lecture 9). The two model-based origins return, now standing on data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The thesis --- learn the dynamics, then the two parents return}
The fixed point of this lecture, in one line:
\begin{center}\large
\alert{Learn the dynamics from data, and the model-based machinery of both lineages comes back to work on top of it.}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
This is the course's reunion. Four chapters have circled one problem:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item DP (Ch 7) and optimal control (Ch 9) \emph{had} a model and computed;
\item value-based (Ch 8) and policy-based RL (Ch 10) \emph{lacked} one and sampled.
\end{itemize}
Model-based RL \alert{learns} $f_\theta$ and lets them collaborate: plan with the model, control with the model, and --- most strikingly --- have an optimal-control \emph{teacher} train a policy \emph{student}.
\medskip

\pause
The price is \alert{model bias}: errors in $f_\theta$ compound through planning. Managing that trade --- sample efficiency vs.\ model error --- is the engineering of the lecture.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The roadmap --- four questions}
\qstrip{0}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
\item \textbf{Q1 --- Why bring a model back at all?} Sample efficiency --- and the spectrum it opens.
\item \textbf{Q2 --- How do we use a learned model?} \alert{Planning} / MPC over $f_\theta$ --- optimal control on learned dynamics.
\item \textbf{Q3 --- Can the planner itself be trained?} \alert{Differentiable MPC} --- backprop through the optimizer.
\item \textbf{Q4 --- How do the lineages reunite?} \alert{Guided policy search} --- control teaches a policy.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 1 --- the spectrum of model use}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{A spectrum, not a binary --- how much to lean on the model}
\qstrip{1}
\medskip
``Model-based'' is a dial, from learning a policy directly to baking the dynamics into the architecture:
\medskip
\begin{center}\scriptsize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c}
 & \textbf{Model-free RL} & \textbf{Model-based RL} & \textbf{Differentiable MPC} \\
\hline
explicit model? & no & yes & yes \\
trained object & policy $\pi(\cdot)$ & model $f(\cdot)$ & model $f(\cdot)$ (and cost) \\
loss & task loss & model-prediction loss & \alert{task loss} \\
execution & policy & planning & planning \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
Reading left to right: more of the world's structure is made explicit, buying \alert{sample efficiency} and generalization. Reading right to left: less to get wrong, dodging \alert{model bias}.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
The deep tension: a learned model is reusable but \emph{wrong}; a model-free policy is honest but \emph{hungry}.
\end{center}
The methods below place themselves along this dial --- and the rightmost, differentiable MPC, even trains the model on the \emph{task} loss rather than a prediction loss, so the model is wrong in whatever way helps the task least.
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 2 --- planning with a learned model}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Planning over a learned model --- MPC}
\qstrip{2}
\medskip
With a learned model $f_\theta$, control becomes \alert{Model Predictive Control}: at each step, optimize a short horizon of actions, execute the first, re-plan.
\[
\tau^*_{1:T} = \mathrm{MPC}(x_{\text{init}}; C, f_\theta) = \arg\min_{x_{1:T}, u_{1:T}} \sum_{t=1}^T C_t(x_t, u_t)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad x_{t+1} = f_\theta(x_t, u_t),\; x_1 = x_{\text{init}}.
\]
\medskip

\pause
This is \alert{Lecture 9's optimal control, run on a learned model} instead of a known one. The model is fit, usually, by minimizing prediction error $L(x_{t+1}, f_\theta(x_t,u_t))$ --- \emph{task-independent, data-dependent}.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
The learned $f_\theta$ is a \alert{simulator}: plan in imagination, act in reality.
\end{center}
\medskip
This is exactly how the wind-farm / furnace controllers of Lecture 1 are deployed online: a learned predictive model inside an MPC solver, re-optimized every step. But fitting the model to \emph{prediction} error has a flaw --- it spends accuracy where the task may not need it.
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 3 --- differentiable control}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Differentiable MPC --- train the planner on the task}
\qstrip{3}
\medskip
\textbf{The idea.} Treat the planner $\mathrm{MPC}(\cdot)$ as a \alert{policy class} and train its model (and cost) \emph{end-to-end on the task loss}, not on prediction error:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x\big\|\,u_{1:T}(x;\theta) - u_{1:T}(x;\hat\theta)\,\big\|^2 \quad(\text{e.g.\ imitate an expert controller } \hat\theta).
\]
\medskip

\pause
\textbf{The obstacle.} Is the $\arg\min$ inside MPC differentiable? For a (convex) QP / LQR subproblem --- \alert{yes}. State the KKT conditions of the planning problem and differentiate \emph{them}:
\[
\text{Lagrangian } L(\tau,\lambda) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{differentiate KKT} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial \tau^*}{\partial \theta}.
\]
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
We backprop \alert{through} an optimizer --- the trick foreshadowed in Lecture 1's KKT backup.
\end{center}
For nonlinear dynamics/cost, iterate iLQR to a fixed point, then linearize and differentiate the resulting LQR (cheaper than unrolling iLQR). \paper{Amos et al., 2018; OptNet}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why differentiable control matters --- and when}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textbf{Imitation learning.} When the goal is to match an expert policy $\hat\pi$ --- not just minimize a known cost --- a differentiable controller lets you fit $\theta$ so $\pi_\theta(s)\approx\hat\pi(s)$, inheriting the structure (stability, constraint-handling) of a controller while learning from demonstrations.
\item \textbf{Structure as inductive bias.} The dynamics constraint $x_{t+1}=f_\theta(x_t,u_t)$ is baked into the architecture --- so far less data is needed than a generic policy network, and extrapolation improves.
\item \textbf{Task-aligned models.} Training on the task loss yields a model that is accurate \emph{where the task needs it}, sidestepping the ``prediction-accurate but control-useless'' failure of Act 2.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
The controller becomes a \alert{differentiable layer} --- optimal control, with learnable parts.
\end{center}
\medskip
\pause
{\footnotesize\textcolor{mLightBrown}{This is the most literal fusion of the two lineages: a control-theory object (MPC/LQR) trained by the gradient-based learning of the data-driven side.}}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 4 --- the lineages teach each other}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Guided Policy Search --- control as a teacher}
\qstrip{4}
\medskip
The clearest reunion: let an \alert{optimal-control teacher} generate good trajectories, and train a \alert{neural policy student} to imitate them. \paper{Levine \& Koltun; Levine et al.}
\medskip

Solve a constrained problem --- optimize trajectories \emph{and} fit a policy, tied together:
\[
\min_{\theta,\, p(\tau)}\ \mathbb{E}_{p(\tau)}[\text{cost}] \quad \text{s.t.}\quad p(u_t\mid x_t) = \pi_\theta(u_t\mid x_t),
\]
solved by alternation (dual gradient descent on the coupling):
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{trajectory optimization} --- improve $p(\tau)$ with iLQR (the optimal-control teacher, using local linear-Gaussian dynamics);
\item \textbf{supervised policy fit} --- train $\pi_\theta$ to match the teacher's trajectories;
\item \textbf{dual update} --- adjust multipliers so policy and trajectories agree.
\end{enumerate}
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
\alert{Optimal control (Lecture 9) supplies the answers; a policy (Lecture 10) learns to reproduce them.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The reunion, and an old friend returns}
GPS turns policy learning into \emph{supervised} learning against an optimal-control oracle --- so a complex policy can be trained with far fewer samples than model-free search, exploiting the local linear-Gaussian structure the controller provides.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{block}{The whole of Part IV, in one mechanism}
\centering
DP / optimal control (model-based) $\xrightarrow{\text{teaches}}$ value / policy (data-driven), \\
on top of a \alert{learned} dynamics model. The four chapters become one system.
\end{block}
\medskip

\pause
\textbf{And design optimization returns.} Many real problems are \alert{bilevel}: an upper-level \emph{design} (turbine layout, furnace geometry) wrapping lower-level \emph{control} problems --- exactly the wind-farm / furnace structure of Lectures 1 and 5. Differentiable control closes the loop: differentiate the optimal control through to the design, and optimize both together.
\[
\min_{p}\ \sum_t \mathcal{L}(x_{t+1}, u_t^*; p) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad u^*_{0:T-1} = \arg\min_{u}\textstyle\sum_t \mathcal{L}(x_{t+1},u_t;p),\ x_{t+1}=f(x_t,u_t;p).
\]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 1 --- differentiating the LQR / QP argmin}
\footnotesize
For a (convex) QP planning subproblem $\;\min_z \tfrac12 z^\top Q z + q^\top z\ \text{s.t.}\ Az = b$, the optimum is characterized by its KKT system:
\[
Q z^* + q + A^\top \nu^* = 0, \qquad A z^* - b = 0.
\]
Rather than differentiate $z^*$ directly, take \emph{differentials} of the KKT conditions:
\[
\begin{bmatrix} Q & A^\top \\ A & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} dz \\ d\nu \end{bmatrix} = -\begin{bmatrix} dQ\,z^* + dq + dA^\top \nu^* \\ dA\,z^* - db \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Solving this linear system yields $\partial z^*/\partial(\cdot)$ for every problem parameter --- so the planner is a differentiable layer. \textbf{LQR specialization.} The finite-horizon LQR optimum is exactly the solution of such a KKT system (with $\tau=(x,u)$); its backward pass has the same cost as one Riccati sweep, far cheaper than unrolling the solver. \paper{OptNet; differentiable MPC}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 2 --- guided policy search, the constrained program}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Objective.} Minimize trajectory cost while forcing the trajectory distribution to agree with the policy:
\[
\min_{\theta,\,p(\tau)} \ \mathbb{E}_{p(\tau)}\Big[\textstyle\sum_t c(x_t,u_t)\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad p(u_t\mid x_t) = \pi_\theta(u_t\mid x_t)\ \forall t.
\]
\textbf{Lagrangian \& alternation.} Form $\mathcal{L}_{\text{GPS}}(\theta, p, \lambda)$ and minimize by turns:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{w.r.t.\ $p(\tau)$} --- trajectory optimization (iLQR under local linear-Gaussian dynamics); the LQR structure makes the local optimal controller $p(u_t\mid x_t)$ fall out automatically;
\item \textbf{w.r.t.\ $\theta$} --- supervised regression: minimize the weighted KL between $\pi_\theta$ and the trajectory controllers;
\item \textbf{dual variables $\lambda$} --- updated (or scheduled) by dual gradient descent to enforce agreement.
\end{enumerate}
\medskip
\textbf{Why it is sample-efficient.} Time-varying linear-Gaussian dynamics are a strong, reasonable local assumption for physical systems, so each trajectory is optimized with far fewer samples than general model-free search --- the gain from injecting control-theoretic structure.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 3 --- the sample-efficiency vs.\ model-bias trade}
\footnotesize
Why model-based RL is powerful \emph{and} dangerous, made precise.
\medskip
\textbf{The gain.} A learned model can be queried without environment interaction, so one real transition informs the value/policy at \emph{many} imagined states (planning, Dyna-style rollouts). Empirically this can cut the required real samples by orders of magnitude --- decisive when interaction is costly (robots, processes, patients).
\medskip
\textbf{The hazard.} Planning trusts $f_\theta$. Where $f_\theta$ is wrong, the planner optimizes a fiction --- and errors \alert{compound} over a horizon (each step's error feeds the next). This is the dynamic cousin of Lecture 5's overestimation trap: an optimizer exploiting a model's blind spots.
\medskip
\textbf{Mitigations.} Short planning horizons; model ensembles / uncertainty (plan pessimistically where the model disagrees with itself); training the model on the \emph{task} loss (differentiable MPC) so it is accurate where it matters; and re-planning often (MPC) so errors are corrected by fresh state. The through-line of the course: \alert{respect the model's uncertainty, or it will be weaponized against you.}
\end{frame}
