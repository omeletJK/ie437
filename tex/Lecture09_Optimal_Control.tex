\section{The handoff --- the second parent}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Where we are --- the other column of the map}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{r|c|c}
 & \textbf{OR / Dynamic Programming} & \textbf{Control Theory} \\
\hline
\textbf{Model-based (origin)}  & MDP \& DP \scriptsize(Lec.\ 7) & \cellcolor{mDarkTeal!20}\textbf{Optimal Control} \scriptsize(\alert{Lec.\ 9})\\
\hline
\textbf{Data-driven (extension)} & Value-Based RL \scriptsize(Lec.\ 8) & Policy-Based RL \scriptsize(Lec.\ 10)\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\smallskip

Sequential decision-making has \alert{two parents}. Lecture 7 gave the first: operations research, dynamic programming, the Bellman equation over discrete states. This lecture introduces the second: \alert{control theory}.
\smallskip

\pause
This is \emph{not} a continuation of value-based RL. It is an independent root. It solves the \alert{same dynamic decision problem} as Lecture 7 --- but in the language of differential equations, continuous state and control, and feedback laws. Lecture 10 will then do to this lecture exactly what Lecture 8 did to Lecture 7: \alert{delete the model and learn from data}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Same problem, different dialect}
The fixed point of this lecture, in one line:
\begin{center}\large
\alert{Control theory and dynamic programming are two dialects for one dynamic optimum.}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
Where Lecture 7 had a stochastic transition over discrete states, control theory studies a (typically continuous, often deterministic) system driven by a control:
\[
\dot x(t) = f\big(x(t), u(t)\big) \quad\text{or}\quad x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t), \qquad \text{minimize } \int g\,dt + q(x_T).
\]
\medskip

\pause
The goal is identical to Lecture 7's: a \alert{feedback law} $u = \gamma(t,x)$ --- the best control at every state and time. The differences are of \emph{tradition}, not of \emph{substance}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item discrete $\to$ \textbf{continuous} state and action;
\item probabilistic transition $T$ $\to$ \textbf{differential} dynamics $f$;
\item sum $\to$ \textbf{integral}; $\max$-backup $\to$ a \textbf{PDE} (the HJB equation).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The translation table --- Lecture 7 in continuous time}
Every object of dynamic programming has a control-theoretic twin.
\medskip
\begin{center}\scriptsize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.55}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l}
\textbf{Lecture 7 (OR / DP)} & \textbf{Lecture 9 (Control)} & \textbf{The shift} \\
\hline
transition $T(s,a,s')$ & dynamics $\dot x = f(x,u)$ & probabilistic $\to$ differential \\
value $V(s)$, $Q(s,a)$ & cost-to-go $V(t,x)$ & state $\to$ state-and-time \\
policy $\pi(s)$ & feedback law $u=\gamma(t,x)$, $u=Kx$ & a rule, now continuous \\
Bellman optimality (sum, $\max$) & HJB equation (PDE, $\min$) & discrete backup $\to$ PDE \\
value iteration / policy iteration & Riccati / Pontryagin & solver, not sampler \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
Read it as one claim: \alert{HJB is the Bellman optimality equation made infinitesimal.} Lecture 7's intuition transfers wholesale; only the calculus changes. Keep the last column in mind --- it is the seam where, in Lecture 10, samples will replace the solver.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The roadmap --- four questions}
One question per Act. This strip returns at every transition.
\qstrip{0}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
\item \textbf{Q1 --- Is this really the same problem?} Discrete-time optimal control \emph{is} Lecture 7's DP, deterministic and continuous-valued.
\item \textbf{Q2 --- What is Bellman in continuous time?} The \alert{Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman} PDE --- powerful, and usually unsolvable. \paper{Bellman}
\item \textbf{Q3 --- When \emph{can} we solve it?} The one closed form: \alert{LQR} and the Riccati equation. \paper{Kalman, 1960}
\item \textbf{Q4 --- Is there another way in?} \alert{Pontryagin's minimum principle} --- the trajectory view. \paper{Pontryagin, 1956}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 1 --- it is the same problem}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Discrete-time optimal control --- DP, deterministic}
\qstrip{1}
\medskip
Choose a control sequence to minimize an accumulated cost subject to deterministic dynamics:
\[
L(u) = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} g_k(x_k,u_k) + g_K(x_K), \qquad x_{k+1} = f_k(x_k,u_k), \quad u_k = \gamma_k(x_k).
\]
\medskip

\pause
Define the cost-to-go $V(k,x)$. The \alert{principle of optimality} --- identical to Lecture 7's --- gives the recursion:
\[
V(k,x) = \min_{u_k}\Big\{\, g_k(x,u_k) + V\big(k{+}1,\, f_k(x,u_k)\big)\,\Big\}.
\]
\medskip

\pause
This is Lecture 7's Bellman backup with two cosmetic changes: the expectation $\sum_{s'}T[\cdots]$ collapses to a single deterministic successor $f_k(x,u_k)$, and states/controls are continuous. \alert{The logic is unchanged} --- solve backward in time, optimal sub-plans compose into an optimal plan. The two traditions agree here exactly; they diverge only when we pass to continuous time.
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 2 --- Bellman made infinitesimal: HJB}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{The continuous-time problem --- and the HJB equation}
\qstrip{2}
\medskip
Now time is continuous:
\[
L(u) = \int_0^T g\big(t,x(t),u(t)\big)\,dt + q\big(T,x(T)\big), \qquad \dot x = f(t,x,u),\; x(0)=x_0.
\]
Let $V(t,x)$ be the cost-to-go. Applying the principle of optimality over an infinitesimal step yields the \alert{Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman} equation:
\[
-\,\partial_t V(t,x) = \min_{u\in U}\Big\{\, \partial_x V(t,x)\, f(t,x,u) + g(t,x,u)\,\Big\}, \qquad V(T,x)=q(T,x).
\]
\medskip

\pause
The optimal feedback reads off pointwise:
\[
u^*(t) = \arg\min_{u\in U}\Big\{\, \partial_x V(t,x)\, f(t,x,u) + g(t,x,u)\,\Big\}.
\]
\begin{center}\large
This is the Bellman optimality equation --- now a nonlinear PDE for $V$.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{HJB --- sufficient, global, and (usually) unsolvable}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textbf{Sufficient \& global.} A smooth $V$ solving HJB over the whole state space \emph{certifies} optimality and yields feedback $\gamma(t,x)$ --- the best action at every $(t,x)$, credible from any state.
\item \textbf{Extends to stochastic} control with barely a change (an extra second-derivative term).
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\pause
But the cost is severe:
\begin{center}\large
\alert{HJB is a nonlinear PDE over the entire state space.}
\end{center}
It needs the model $f,g$, assumes $V$ is differentiable, and does not survive high dimensions --- the value field over $x$ is exponentially large (the same curse Lecture 7's table faced, now continuous).
\medskip

\pause
So HJB is the \emph{characterization}, rarely the \emph{computation}. It is exactly solvable in essentially one case --- the case every control engineer knows cold.
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 3 --- the one closed form: LQR}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{LQR --- linear dynamics, quadratic cost}
\qstrip{3}
\medskip
The harmonic oscillator of control:
\[
\dot x = Ax + Bu, \qquad J = \int_0^T \big(x^\top Q x + u^\top R u\big)\,dt + x_T^\top Q_f x_T.
\]
\textbf{Ansatz} $V(t,x) = x^\top P_t\, x$. Substitute into HJB; the inner minimization is now a simple quadratic in $u$:
\[
u^*(t) = \underbrace{-R^{-1}B^\top P_t}_{\textstyle =\,-K_t}\, x(t), \qquad
-\dot P_t = A^\top P_t + P_t A - P_t B R^{-1} B^\top P_t + Q.
\]
\medskip

\pause
The last is the \alert{Riccati differential equation}. Infinite horizon $\Rightarrow$ $P$ constant, solving the algebraic Riccati equation; the controller becomes a fixed gain:
\begin{center}\large
\alert{the optimal controller is a matrix: $u^* = -Kx$.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why LQR is the cornerstone}
LQR is not just \emph{a} solvable case --- it is the workhorse the rest of control (and much of RL) leans on.
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textbf{Linear feedback, globally optimal} --- no iteration, no approximation, for the LQ problem.
\item \textbf{The local model of everything.} Any smooth nonlinear problem, linearized about a trajectory with a quadratic cost approximation, becomes LQR --- this is \alert{iLQR / DDP}, the backbone of trajectory optimization and model-based planning.
\item \textbf{The target the data-driven world reaches for.} In Lecture 10, DDPG's learned actor $\mu_\theta(s)$ plays the role of this gain $K$ --- when $A,B$ are \emph{unknown}.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
Hold $u = Kx$ in view: it is the closed-form answer that \alert{policy gradient learns to approximate blind.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 4 --- the other view: Pontryagin}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Pontryagin's minimum principle --- follow one trajectory}
\qstrip{4}
\medskip
HJB is the \emph{dynamic-programming} (Eulerian) view: carry a value field over all states. There is a second, \emph{Lagrangian} view: enforce the dynamics as a constraint along a single optimal trajectory.
\medskip

Form the \alert{Hamiltonian} with a costate (Lagrange multiplier) $\lambda(t)$:
\[
H(t,x,u,\lambda) = g(t,x,u) + \lambda^\top f(t,x,u).
\]
\pause
Along the optimum, necessary conditions hold:
\[
\dot x^* = \partial_\lambda H = f, \qquad
\dot\lambda^* = -\,\partial_x H, \qquad
\lambda(T) = \partial_x q\big(T,x^*(T)\big), \qquad
u^* = \arg\min_{u\in U} H(t,x^*,u,\lambda^*).
\]
\medskip

\pause
Dynamic optimization is reduced to a \alert{static} minimization of $H$ at each instant --- the same ``freeze time, optimize pointwise'' move that recurs throughout the course. The costate $\lambda$ is the trajectory-space shadow of $\partial_x V$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{HJB vs.\ Pontryagin --- two solvers, one optimum}
\begin{center}\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l}
 & \textbf{HJB (dynamic programming)} & \textbf{Pontryagin (minimum principle)} \\
\hline
View & Eulerian: a value \emph{field} $V(t,x)$ & Lagrangian: one \emph{trajectory} $x^*(t)$ \\
Logic & \alert{sufficient}, over all $(t,x)$ & \alert{necessary}, along the optimum \\
Output & feedback law $\gamma(t,x)$ & open-loop $u^*(t)$ (a costate ODE) \\
Math & nonlinear \alert{PDE} & two-point boundary-value \alert{ODE} \\
Stochastic & extends naturally & does not, in general \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
Feedback ($\gamma(t,x)$) beats an open-loop plan ($u^*(t)$): it stays valid from \emph{any} state you actually reach, not just the one you predicted. That preference --- a \emph{rule}, not a fragile pre-printed plan --- is the same one Lecture 7 expressed, and the one reinforcement learning will inherit.
\medskip
\pause
{\footnotesize\textcolor{mLightBrown}{Pontryagin's machinery --- Lagrangian, costate, trajectory optimization --- returns in Lecture 10 (REINFORCE's trajectory view) and Lecture 11 (guided policy search imitating an iLQR teacher).}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 1 --- the HJB equation, derived in one step}
\footnotesize
Start from Bellman's optimality principle over a short interval $\delta$:
\[
V(t,x) = \min_{u\in U}\Big\{\, g(t,x,u)\,\delta + V\big(t+\delta,\, x + f(t,x,u)\,\delta\big)\,\Big\}.
\]
Taylor-expand the second term:
\[
V\big(t+\delta, x + f\delta\big) = V(t,x) + \partial_t V(t,x)\,\delta + \partial_x V(t,x)\, f(t,x,u)\,\delta + o(\delta).
\]
Substitute, cancel $V(t,x)$, divide by $\delta$, let $\delta\to0$:
\[
0 = \min_{u\in U}\Big\{ g(t,x,u) + \partial_t V + \partial_x V\, f(t,x,u)\Big\}
\;\Rightarrow\;
-\partial_t V = \min_{u} \big\{\partial_x V\, f + g\big\}. \qquad\blacksquare
\]
\medskip
The minimizing $u$ at each $(t,x)$ is the optimal feedback --- a static pointwise minimization embedded in a PDE for $V$. Compare Lecture 7, Backup 1: the same derivation with $\delta\to0$ instead of a one-step sum.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 2 --- LQR: the Riccati derivation}
\footnotesize
System $\dot x = Ax+Bu$, cost density $g = x^\top Q x + u^\top R u$. Guess $V(t,x)=x^\top P_t x$, so $\partial_x V = 2P_t x$, $\partial_t V = x^\top \dot P_t x$. HJB:
\[
-x^\top \dot P_t x = \min_u\Big\{\, 2 x^\top P_t (Ax + Bu) + x^\top Q x + u^\top R u \,\Big\}.
\]
\textbf{Minimize over $u$:} $2B^\top P_t x + 2R u = 0 \Rightarrow u^* = -R^{-1}B^\top P_t\, x$.
\medskip

Substitute back; matching $x^\top(\cdot)x$ for all $x$ yields the \alert{Riccati differential equation}
\[
-\dot P_t = A^\top P_t + P_t A - P_t B R^{-1} B^\top P_t + Q.
\]
Infinite horizon: $\dot P\to0$, giving the \alert{algebraic Riccati equation} $A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0$, unique PSD solution $\Rightarrow$ constant gain $K = R^{-1}B^\top P$, $u^* = -Kx$.
\medskip
\textbf{Discrete-time analogue:} $K = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1} B^\top P A$, with $P$ the fixed point of the discrete Riccati recursion --- the form a learned critic implicitly targets in Lecture 10.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 3 --- Euler--Lagrange to Pontryagin}
\footnotesize
Adjoin the dynamics to the cost with a costate $\lambda(t)$, forming the Hamiltonian $H = g + \lambda^\top f$:
\[
\bar J = \int_0^T \big[ H(t,x,u,\lambda) - \lambda^\top \dot x \big]\,dt + q(x(T)).
\]
Perturb the optimal control $u^* \to u^* + \epsilon\, p(t)$, require $\tfrac{d\bar J}{d\epsilon}\big|_{0}=0$ for all admissible $p$. Integrating by parts and collecting terms gives the necessary conditions:
\[
\dot x^* = \partial_\lambda H = f, \qquad
\dot\lambda^* = -\partial_x H, \qquad
\lambda(T) = \partial_x q, \qquad
\partial_u H = 0.
\]
\textbf{Euler--Lagrange $\to$ minimum principle.} Euler--Lagrange assumes $u$ is interior and the stationarity $\partial_u H = 0$ holds. Pontryagin generalizes to a constrained set $u\in U$: stationarity is replaced by the global condition $u^* = \arg\min_{u\in U} H$, which remains valid at the boundary of $U$ (bang-bang controls, saturation).
\medskip
\textbf{Why we keep it.} The minimum principle is the standard way to \emph{state} equilibrium (necessary) conditions for dynamic games --- which is exactly how it is used in Lectures 11--12.
\end{frame}
