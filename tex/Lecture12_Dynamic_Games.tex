\section{The handoff --- a second decision maker}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Where we are --- the last axis}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{center}\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabular}{r|c|c}
 & \textbf{Single agent} & \textbf{Multi agent} \\
\hline
\textbf{Static}  & optimization \scriptsize(Lec.\ 1) & static game \\
\hline
\textbf{Dynamic} & RL / control \scriptsize(Lec.\ 7--11) & \cellcolor{mDarkTeal!20}\textbf{dynamic game} \scriptsize(\alert{Lec.\ 12} $\to$ 13)\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\smallskip

Every lecture so far --- optimization, Bayesian inference, design, MDPs, control, RL --- assumed \alert{one decision maker} pursuing \alert{one objective}. We now cross the final axis: \alert{single $\to$ multi-agent}.
\smallskip

\pause
And we cross it the same way we crossed ``static $\to$ dynamic'': \alert{model-based first}. This lecture is the multi-agent \emph{parallel of Lecture 9} --- the model-based, control-theoretic foundation. Just as optimal control (Lec.\ 9) was made data-driven into policy RL (Lec.\ 10), this dynamic-game theory will be made data-driven into multi-agent RL (Lec.\ 13).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The thesis --- ``optimal'' dissolves; ``equilibrium'' replaces it}
The fixed point of this lecture, in one line:
\begin{center}\large
\alert{With many self-interested agents there is no single ``optimal'' --- only an equilibrium, and optimal control is the tool that characterizes it.}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
The trouble is immediate. Each agent $i$ has its own cost $L^i$ depending on \emph{everyone's} actions $u^1,\dots,u^N$. Agent $i$ cannot minimize $L^i$ alone --- its best move depends on what the others do, whose best moves depend on what $i$ does. The objectives are \alert{coupled}; ``minimize'' is no longer well-posed.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
The solution concept must shift: from an \alert{optimum} (best in isolation) to an \alert{equilibrium} (no one gains by deviating alone).
\end{center}
\medskip
And remarkably, the tool that pins down such an equilibrium is the one we already built: \alert{optimal control}. An equilibrium is a profile at which \emph{each agent is optimal-controlling against the others}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The roadmap --- four questions}
\qstrip{0}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
\item \textbf{Q1 --- Why can't we just optimize?} Coupled objectives; the need for an \alert{equilibrium}.
\item \textbf{Q2 --- What is the equilibrium?} \alert{Nash} (and its kin) --- and its tie to optimal control.
\item \textbf{Q3 --- What changes over time?} \alert{Dynamic games} --- coupled HJB, feedback Nash. \paper{Ba\c{s}ar \& Olsder}
\item \textbf{Q4 --- Can we solve it?} The \alert{gap} --- characterized, rarely computable --- that Lecture 13 will close.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 1 --- from optimum to equilibrium}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Coupled objectives --- the static game}
\qstrip{1}
\medskip
$N$ agents; agent $i$ chooses $u^i$ and bears cost $L^i(u^1,\dots,u^N)$. In \emph{normal form}, everyone moves at once.
\medskip

There is no joint ``minimum'': lowering $L^1$ may raise $L^2$. Instead we ask for stability against unilateral change. A profile $(u^{1*},\dots,u^{N*})$ is a \alert{Nash equilibrium} if, for every agent $i$,
\[
L^i(u^{1*},\dots,u^{i*},\dots,u^{N*}) \;\le\; L^i(u^{1*},\dots,u^{i},\dots,u^{N*}) \quad \forall u^i.
\]
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
\alert{No agent can improve its own cost by changing only its own action.}
\end{center}
\medskip
Each line of the definition fixes everyone else at their equilibrium choice and says agent $i$ is already best-responding. Equilibrium is a \alert{mutual best response} --- a fixed point of the ``everyone reacts to everyone'' map.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A menu of equilibrium concepts}
``Equilibrium'' is not one thing --- the modeler chooses which, by the structure of the game:
\medskip
\begin{center}\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{l|l}
\textbf{Concept} & \textbf{When} \\
\hline
\textbf{Nash} & general non-cooperative; mutual best response \\
\textbf{Zero-sum (minimax)} & one's gain is another's loss (saddle point) \\
\textbf{Stackelberg} & a leader commits first, followers respond \\
\textbf{Correlated} & a shared signal coordinates the agents \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
The game \emph{type} --- cooperative, competitive, mixed --- enters entirely through the per-agent costs $L^i$. The \emph{concept} is a modeling choice, exactly as picking an objective was in Lecture 1.
\medskip

\pause
{\footnotesize\textcolor{mLightBrown}{Hold this menu in view: in Lecture 13 the modeler still \emph{chooses} the concept, but a learning algorithm \emph{converges to} it --- the choice's meaning shifts from ``solve by hand'' to ``provably reach.''}}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 2 --- equilibrium through optimal control}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{The key bridge --- a Nash equilibrium is coupled optimal control}
\qstrip{2}
\medskip
\begin{block}{Optimal control is a one-player game}
\centering
An optimal control problem is exactly a dynamic game with \alert{one player and one criterion}.
\end{block}
\medskip

\pause
Run that backward. At a Nash equilibrium, \alert{freeze every other agent} at its equilibrium strategy. Then agent $i$ faces a problem with a single objective $L^i$ and the others' actions baked into its environment --- \alert{an optimal control problem}.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
A Nash equilibrium is a profile where \alert{every agent is simultaneously solving its own optimal control problem} against the others.
\end{center}
\medskip
\pause
So the tools of Lecture 9 --- HJB, the minimum principle, Riccati --- are exactly the tools to \emph{state} and \emph{verify} equilibrium. To check a profile is Nash, we check each agent's optimal-control conditions hold with the others fixed. \alert{This is why we learned optimal control.}
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 3 --- the dynamic game}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{Dynamic games --- coupling through a shared state}
\qstrip{3}
\medskip
Now let the agents share a dynamical system and act over time --- a \alert{differential game} (continuous) or \alert{Markov / stochastic game} (discrete):
\[
\dot x = f(x, u^1,\dots,u^N), \qquad J^i = \int_0^T g^i(x, u^1,\dots,u^N)\,dt + q^i(x_T).
\]
\medskip

\pause
\textbf{Information structure matters now} --- what each agent knows when it acts:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{open-loop:} commit to $u^i(t)$ up front, knowing only $x_0$;
\item \textbf{feedback:} react to the current state, $u^i = \gamma^i(t, x)$.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
Unlike single-agent control, \alert{open-loop and feedback Nash equilibria differ} ---
\end{center}
because what others can \emph{observe and react to} changes what is a best response. Feedback Nash, like Lecture 9's feedback law, is the credible one: \alert{subgame-perfect}, valid from any state actually reached.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Coupled HJB --- Bellman, once per player}
Apply Lecture 9's dynamic programming to \emph{each} agent, with the others' feedback strategies substituted into the dynamics and cost. We get $N$ \alert{coupled HJB equations}:
\[
-\partial_t V^i = \min_{u^i}\Big\{ \partial_x V^i\, f(x, u^i, \gamma^{-i}(t,x)) + g^i(x, u^i, \gamma^{-i}(t,x)) \Big\}, \quad i = 1,\dots,N,
\]
with $\gamma^{-i}$ the others' equilibrium strategies. The minimizer gives agent $i$'s feedback $\gamma^i(t,x)$.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
$N$ Bellman equations, each minimizing over its \alert{own} slot, all \alert{coupled} through the shared $\gamma^{-i}$.
\end{center}
\medskip

\pause
A feedback Nash equilibrium is a \emph{joint solution} of this coupled system. (The minimum principle gives the parallel necessary conditions --- $N$ coupled Hamiltonians, one costate per agent --- the standard way to \emph{state} dynamic-game equilibria.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The solvable case --- linear-quadratic games and coupled Riccati}
As in Lecture 9, the one clean closed form is linear-quadratic. Linear shared dynamics, quadratic per-agent costs $\Rightarrow$ each $V^i = x^\top P^i x$, and the coupled HJB collapses to \alert{coupled Riccati equations}:
\[
u^{i*} = -(R^{ii})^{-1} (B^i)^\top P^i\, x = -K^i x, \qquad \{P^i\}_{i=1}^N \text{ solve } N \text{ coupled Riccati equations}.
\]
\medskip

\pause
Each agent's gain $K^i$ depends on \emph{all} the others' $P^j$ --- the Riccati equations are \alert{intertwined}, not separate. Solve them jointly and you have the feedback Nash equilibrium in closed form: each agent a linear controller, mutually best-responding.
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
This ``coupled Riccati'' is the multi-agent echo of Lecture 9's single Riccati ---
\end{center}
and the exact target Lecture 13 will reach \emph{without} the model, by learning. (The subtitle of the next lecture: \emph{from coupled Riccati to provable equilibrium learning}.)
\end{frame}

%==========================================================
\section{Act 4 --- the gap}
%==========================================================

\begin{frame}{The wall --- the equations exist, the solutions don't}
\qstrip{4}
\medskip
We can \emph{characterize} dynamic-game equilibria. We usually cannot \emph{compute} them. Three walls --- the same three that motivated RL, now harder:
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
\item \textbf{The model.} Coupled HJB needs $f$ and every $g^i$. Real multi-agent systems rarely hand them over.
\item \textbf{The closed form.} Outside linear-quadratic, the coupled equations are \alert{circular} --- each agent's solution depends on the others' unsolved solutions. No general formula.
\item \textbf{The dimension.} Even numerically, $N$ coupled value fields over a shared $x$ do not survive high dimensions.
\end{itemize}
\medskip

\pause
\begin{center}\large
\alert{The equilibrium conditions exist. The equilibria don't --- not computably.}
\end{center}
\medskip
\pause
That gap is the entire reason for the next lecture. Replace ``solve the coupled equations'' with \alert{``learn from data''} --- and ask what the learned equilibrium can \emph{guarantee}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 1 --- Nash, formally, and best-response}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Nash equilibrium.} A profile $(u^{1*},\dots,u^{N*})$ such that for every agent $i$,
\[
L^i(u^{i*}, u^{-i*}) \le L^i(u^{i}, u^{-i*}) \quad \forall u^i, \qquad u^{-i} := (u^j)_{j\ne i}.
\]
\textbf{Best-response map.} Define $\mathrm{BR}^i(u^{-i}) = \arg\min_{u^i} L^i(u^i, u^{-i})$. A Nash equilibrium is a \emph{fixed point} of the joint best-response: $u^{i*} \in \mathrm{BR}^i(u^{-i*})$ for all $i$. This is why existence proofs use fixed-point theorems (Brouwer / Kakutani), and why algorithms that iterate best responses are the natural way to seek equilibria --- a pattern that returns, guarded, as the sequential-update schemes of Lecture 13.
\medskip
\textbf{Mixed strategies.} When no pure-strategy equilibrium exists (e.g.\ matching pennies), agents randomize; a Nash equilibrium in \emph{mixed} strategies always exists for finite games (Nash's theorem). This is why a single deterministic policy can be exploitable --- a point Lecture 13 must confront.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 2 --- why optimal control verifies Nash}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Claim.} Verifying a strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium reduces to $N$ optimal-control problems.
\medskip
\textbf{Argument.} Fix all agents $j\ne i$ at $\gamma^{j*}$. Substitute into the shared dynamics and into $g^i$:
\[
\dot x = f(x, u^i, \gamma^{-i*}(t,x)) =: \tilde f^i(x, u^i), \qquad \tilde g^i(x, u^i) := g^i(x, u^i, \gamma^{-i*}(t,x)).
\]
Agent $i$ now faces a \emph{single-agent} optimal control problem $\min_{u^i}\int \tilde g^i\,dt + q^i$ s.t.\ $\dot x = \tilde f^i$. The profile is Nash iff, for \emph{every} $i$, $\gamma^{i*}$ solves this problem --- i.e.\ satisfies agent $i$'s HJB (sufficient) or minimum principle (necessary). Hence equilibrium analysis \emph{is} optimal control, applied $N$ times with a consistency (fixed-point) requirement linking them. The coupling is precisely that each agent's ``environment'' contains the others' equilibrium strategies.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup 3 --- open-loop vs.\ feedback Nash}
\footnotesize
A subtlety with no single-agent analogue: the \emph{information structure} changes the equilibrium.
\medskip
\begin{center}\scriptsize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l}
 & \textbf{Open-loop Nash} & \textbf{Feedback Nash} \\
\hline
strategy & $u^i(t)$, a function of time & $\gamma^i(t,x)$, a function of state \\
information & $x_0$ only & current $x$ \\
tool & coupled minimum principle (ODEs) & coupled HJB (PDEs) \\
credibility & not subgame-perfect & \alert{subgame-perfect}, time-consistent \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip
\textbf{Why they differ.} In single-agent control, open-loop and feedback give the \emph{same} optimal cost (the optimum is the optimum). In a game they generally do \emph{not}: a feedback strategy lets an agent \emph{threaten to react} to deviations, changing what its rivals find optimal. The ability to condition on state is strategic leverage. \textbf{Preference.} Feedback Nash is the credible target --- valid from any state actually reached --- the same reason Lecture 9 preferred feedback laws to open-loop plans, now with strategic teeth.
\end{frame}
